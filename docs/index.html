2.1 Automated Naturalness

UTMOS · DNSMOS · 200 sentences ?
Understanding This Test

Measures how natural the synthesised speech sounds, with no reference audio required — two learned models score the audio directly.

UTMOS
A neural network trained to predict human Mean Opinion Score on a 1–5 scale. Above 4.0 is considered near-human; below 3.5 sounds noticeably synthetic. This is the strongest single naturalness signal in the suite.
DNSMOS OVRL / SIG / BAK
A three-part MOS estimator: OVRL (overall), SIG (speech-signal quality), BAK (background quietness). It is calibrated on noise-suppressed real speech, so it tends to read lower on pristine synthetic audio — SIG is usually the most relevant sub-score for TTS.
P50 / P90
Median and 90th-percentile scores across the 200 sentences. A high median with a low minimum means most output is excellent with a few weaker sentences.

200 sentences are synthesised once each and scored locally on GPU.

 Good

What: Reference-free perceptual quality scoring of synthesised speech. UTMOS is a learned MOS predictor (1–5 scale, >4.0 = near-human); DNSMOS is a noise-suppression-calibrated MOS estimator with three sub-scores (OVRL overall, SIG signal quality, BAK background quietness).  How: 200 sentences synthesised once each through the REST batch path, then scored locally on GPU. 2.2 Intelligibility — Round-Trip WER

Whisper large-v3 + EnglishTextNormalizer · 230 sentences ?
Understanding This Test

Checks whether the synthesised speech is clear enough to be transcribed back to the original words — a proxy for how intelligible it is.

Round-trip WER
The text is synthesised to audio, then a speech recogniser (Whisper large-v3) transcribes it back. Word Error Rate is the percentage of words the transcript gets wrong versus the original. Clean TTS yields near-zero WER; human-level recognition sits around 5%.
CER
Character Error Rate — the same idea at the character level, catching minor slips WER might over-penalise.
EnglishTextNormalizer
Both sides are canonicalised (digit↔word, currency, contractions, units) before comparison, so a spoken “one thousand” isn't falsely counted wrong against a written “1000”.
Categories
Sentences span Harvard (phonetically balanced), technical jargon, basic numbers, edge-case number notation, and conversational text — to expose where intelligibility holds or slips.
 Pass on 4 / 5 categories

What: Word Error Rate of a Whisper large-v3 transcript against the reference text used to synthesise. Clean TTS produces audio Whisper transcribes back almost exactly — the rate at which it can't is a proxy for intelligibility.  How: Each reference sentence is synthesised through the REST path, transcribed locally with Whisper large-v3, then compared with jiwer. Both sides are normalised with Whisper's EnglishTextNormalizer, which canonicalises digit↔word, currency, contractions and units so spoken-form references aren't falsely penalised against Whisper's digit-form output.

2.3 Prosody (reference-free)

F0 & speaking-rate distribution · 50 sentences ?
Understanding This Test

Characterises the “musicality” of the voice — its pitch and pace — measured directly on the audio without any reference.

F0 (fundamental frequency)
The pitch of the voice in Hz. The mean places the voice (a typical adult female voice sits ~165–220 Hz); the std and range show intonation — too little spread sounds flat/monotone, a natural range carries expression.
Speaking rate (WPM)
Words per minute over voiced audio. Natural conversational speech is roughly 140–200 WPM; the per-sentence spread reflects normal pacing differences across content.

Reference-free: there is no “correct” target — the goal is to confirm pitch and pace fall in a natural, expressive range rather than sounding robotic or rushed.

 Natural range

2.5 Streaming TTFB & RTF

WebSocket vs REST · 5 length buckets × 50 reps ?
Understanding This Test

Measures responsiveness — how fast audio starts, and whether generation keeps ahead of playback — across five input lengths on both interfaces.

TTFB (Time to First Byte)
How long until the first chunk of audio arrives after the request is sent. This is what a user perceives as responsiveness; above ~500 ms starts to feel laggy in conversation.
RTF (Real-Time Factor)
Generation time ÷ audio duration. Below 1.0 means audio is produced faster than it plays — required for real-time streaming. 0.2 means a second of audio is generated in 0.2 s.
P50 / P99
Median and 99th-percentile. A large P50→P99 gap means occasional slow outliers — the tail matters for consistent streaming.
WebSocket vs REST
WebSocket streams chunks as they generate (true first-chunk TTFB); REST reads the response in chunks. Comparing both shows which path gives the more stable latency.
 Real-time capable

What: Time-to-first-byte (how quickly audio starts) and real-time factor (generation time ÷ audio duration; <1.0 means faster than playback), measured across five input lengths on both interfaces.  How: Each bucket run 50× on the WebSocket streaming path (true first-chunk TTFB) and the REST path (chunked read). 2.6 Throughput & Concurrency

N = 1, 5, 10, 20 · 50 requests per level ?
Understanding This Test

Tests how the service behaves under parallel load — does it keep up as more requests arrive at once, and where does it start to push back?

Concurrency (N)
The number of requests in flight simultaneously, simulating N users at once. Run at N = 1, 5, 10, 20.
RPS (requests per second)
Sustained throughput at each level. Ideally it climbs with N until the service saturates.
Latency P50 / P99
Per-request timing under load. Rising latency or a widening P99 signals the service is straining.
Error rate / 429
HTTP 429 “Too Many Requests” is the service rate-limiting the client. A burst of 429s marks the concurrency ceiling — the point beyond which requests should be throttled client-side and retried with backoff.

Two paths are exercised: a synchronous thread-pool and an async REST client.

 Ceiling found at N=20 (REST)

What: Sustained requests-per-second and per-request latency as concurrency rises, on a synchronous thread-pool path and an async REST path.  How: 50 identical requests fired at each concurrency level on both paths; RPS, latency percentiles and error rate recorded.

  • N=1→10 scales cleanly: throughput climbs 0.45 → 2.0 → ~3.7–3.9 RPS with zero errors on both paths and stable per-request latency (~2.5 s). This is the usable concurrency envelope.
  • At N=20 the REST async path hit the rate limit hard — 40 of 50 requests returned HTTP 429 (80% error rate). The reported RPS for that row is not meaningful.   • Practical ceiling: keep sustained concurrency at or below ~10 in-flight requests for this key/tier, or add client-side rate limiting and retry-with-backoff to absorb 429s beyond that.

2.8 Long-Form Voice Consistency

ECAPA-TDNN speaker similarity · 5 multi-paragraph passages ?
Understanding This Test

Checks whether the voice stays the same across a long passage — does its identity, pitch, or pace drift from the first paragraph to the last?

Speaker similarity (ECAPA-TDNN)
Each paragraph is turned into a speaker “fingerprint” (embedding) by ECAPA-TDNN, a speaker-verification model. Paragraphs are compared; a similarity near 1.0 means the same voice throughout, lower values mean the identity is drifting.
F0 drift
How much the average pitch wanders across paragraphs, in Hz. Small drift against the ~187 Hz baseline means the voice stays anchored.
WPM drift
Variation in speaking rate across paragraphs. Modest, content-dependent variation is natural; large swings would signal instability.

Five multi-paragraph passages on different topics are synthesised and analysed paragraph by paragraph.

 Stable

What: Whether the voice stays consistent across a long passage — does timbre, pitch, or pace drift paragraph to paragraph?  How: Five multi-paragraph passages synthesised; each paragraph embedded with ECAPA-TDNN (speaker-similarity), plus per-paragraph F0 and speaking-rate spread.
